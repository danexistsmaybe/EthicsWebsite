## Abstract
For many powerful proprietary software applications, there exists some free version that owes its existence to open source distribution. In a system where any the value of any labor is exploited, this appears as a magical phenomenon; thus, this case study begins by examining the nature of open source distribution and its origins so that it may better be understood. Until recently, the majority of competitive AI technology remained completely closed source, consolidating power under a few key big tech players, but the introduction of the open-weight Deepseek model has caused a marked shift in the AI landscape. Its developers proved that AI can be made more efficient, and, moreover, they made a powerful model that is free to distribute and build with. In addition, the public and academic sectors are racing to catch up with their own open AI technology. Yet, the introduction of "unsecured" models presents significant dangers for the average person, leading to a major debate over something that has previously been taken as objectively good: should AI be made open source? In order to find out, this case study analyzes the effect of open source AI on two of its biggest stakeholders, consumers and developers. Finally, the open source method can be and has been applied to more than just software. Hardware, literature, and even governance have all been considered from within open source frameworks. The electorate must decide whether or not the open source methodology offers a way to approach a more collaborative world, or if it only remains effective in the realm of software.

## Introduction
Across discussions around open source software, particularly from its proponents and contributers, the word *community* comes up quite frequently. Community is, in many ways, a utopian word, a word that invokes all of the most beautiful parts of social relations while somehow navigating the difficulties. At its most abstract, the quality of being open source represents the realization of a modern, serious effort towards that utopian ideal and an indication that technology is useful, perhaps even essential, for getting there. Nevertheless, the first free and open source software (FOSS) was not written with such grand intentions; the reality of open source software takes a much more specific and nuanced form; and critics of such an idea claim that it is unprofitable, vulnerable, and ultimately unsustainable. After providing an interactive history of FOSS, I will examine the most recent development in its timeline, generative AI. I will go on to provide analysis of FOSS from the perspectives of the two biggest stakeholders, consumers and developers. Finally, I will discuss the economic reality of FOSS, and I will then conclude with the notion of open source as a movement and an ideal, ending with an argument that FOSS provides a clear framework towards realizing our collective hopes as a society.

### Background
Before all that, I must provide some background on the case. Traditional models of software distribution rely on the fact that the process of compilation is difficult to reverse and can easily be further obfuscated without affecting performance. In other words, given a piece of compiled machine code, it is impossible to recover its readability without difficult and time-consuming interpretation and refactoring. Thus, for-profit distributors of software have most often released their programs as binary code, which is effective in allowing them to continue monetizing their product, keeping it proprietary, and controlling its use.

In simple terms, free and open source software refers to the decision to distribute software as source code rather than as machine code \[1\]. There are many different, sometimes conflicting motivations for this decision, including philisophical, ethical, economic, and pragmatic reasoning, but, in each case, distributors must determine how and to what extent they will protect their intellectual property. Controlling the source code is most often accomplished through private law, that is, legally binding agreements between creators and consumers like those expressed in common open source software licenses.

### Open Source AI
The most recent development in the area of FOSS is, of course, generative AI. The vast majority of state-of-the-art AI models in the United States have been kept under lock and key, only accessible through web interfaces and API calls. Some have read this as a sign of open source's inability to remain useful in the face of modern developments in computational infrastructure, while others have argued that moving to an open source model for AI development would pose to great of a security risk \[5\]. Either way, it was widely accepted as simple reality that generative AI models only existed behind the closed doors of big tech companies like Google and Meta and well-funded startups like OpenAI and Anthropic. That is, until DeepSeek released its open weight v3 model in December of 2024 \[4\]. The introduction of a free, competitive model from China has significantly disrupted previous narratives around the development of AI, and the industry continues to move forward with many open questions.

Still, in order to understand current discourse over open source AI, it is necessary to understand how FOSS began, what events led to its popularity, and how more recent developments in the software development landscape have changed the way FOSS is performed in industry.

## Open Source AI
Throughout 2024, AI primarily remained closed source, and big tech was making increasingly aggressive moves to secure its investments and dominate the industry. The industry ecosystem was primarily composed of three layers, with smaller AI startups leading the charge, tech giants -- primarily Microsoft, Google, and Amazon -- investing billions and performing the majority of computation, and, finally, a small, open source group of investors and platforms that lag far behind. On the surface, it may appear as though the prominence of smaller startups points to competitive diversity within the artificial intelligence industry, but one event in November of 2023 year demonstrated how this is not true \[2\]. 

In late November, Sam Altman, who remains at the helm as OpenAI's CEO today, was fired by the board and then rehired a few days later. His removal followed from an inherent fission in the company, one that began all the way back in 2019. One year prior, Elon Musk, a prominent backer of the startup, quit the board and stopped his funding. At this point, the company broke away from its non-profit, independent, ideal-focused roots -- at least in part -- and established a for-profit wing that was open to investment. ... Continue to explain how big tech has a strangle-hold on the startups \[10\]. 

The trajectory of the industry seemed to point directly to further consolidation of power by the few big players all the way until the very last month of 2024. It was at this point that DeepSeek released its v3 model and changed the playing field in several profound ways. Of course, it showed that US dominance in the artificial intelligence industry was no longer guaranteed, and DeepSeek's model was trained on approximately one eighth of the computing resources for Chat-GPT, which challenged the notion that only big tech owned the computational resources necessary to participate in new model development. But, perhaps the most significant part of DeepSeek's disruption was the fact that it is open weight \[4\].

While there is significant overlap between software development and machine learning, the two fields are different enough that what it means to be open source is not well-defined for the latter. Because building a model requires, at the end of the day, a significant amount of money for training, making the training source code available does not have the same benefits of possible free contribution and community development that software engineering does. 

> “Because it’s a black box, even if I tell you that LLaMa 2, or GPT-4 for that matter, has a problem with hallucinating, even in a narrow domain, it’s not obvious,” says Bommasani, a professor at Stanford. “I can’t go to line 43 of the code and change something and it’s fixed. First of all, the intervention might require retraining the model, which is much more capital intensive than going to fix some lines of code” \[6\]. 

Thus, models may be open source to various degrees. A fully open source model leaves source code, training datasets, model architecture, and the trained model open to the public, so that the specific methods used to produce that model can be reproduced and examined. An open weight model provides its trained parameters but not the steps nor data that were used to produce those parameters, and this is how DeepSeek is distributed. Specifically, DeepSeek typically uses a licence published by MIT, which is extremely permissive and includes the permission to sell the model \[11\]. 

There are also growing movements from the public and academic sectors to, at the very least, make artificial intelligence a more collaborative industry. For example, in October of 2023, the NSF, under direction from the White House, committed $2.6 billion towards a National Artificial Intelligence Research Resource (NAIRR), with the stated goal of improving open access to the resources necessary for research and development of artificial intelligence. This approach has been more popular in Europe, where the EU has been investing $1 billion annually into the development of European data centers and AI development, which they stipulate "are open for the participation of all organizations and individuals" \[6\]. As previously described, the future and potential of open source AI hinges on the ability for a diversified community to form around development, and this can only happen when access to computing resources is not confined to the servers of three big tech companies. With more efficient models as illustrated by DeepSeek and more public and academic investment in open computing resources, open source AI will continue to become more and more realistic. However, this may be akin to opening up Pandora's box.

### The Dangers of Open Source AI
There are critics of open source AI, and their problem with this way of releasing models is in safety. They argue that, although safety protections on secure, cloud-hosted models like Chat-GPT often are found to have vulnerabilities, "unsecured" models, that is, models that are modifiable and distributable by anyone, would be so much worse. 

> "The threat posed by unsecured AI systems lies in the ease of misuse. They are particularly dangerous in the hands of sophisticated threat actors, who could easily download the original versions of these AI systems and disable their safety features, then make their own custom versions and abuse them for a wide variety of tasks" \[5\].

This poses problems in all of the areas that AI can be used unethically: influencing elections, deepfake pornography, and easy access to sensitive information like making biological weapons. Proponents of this viewpoint recommend not only stepping away from open source AI, but also actively regulating against it. They want the developers of sophisticated AI systems to be able to be held liable. The question is, how does the result of this debate affect AI's major stakeholders?


## Stakeholder 1: Consumers
### Open Source and the AI Bubble
The artificial intelligence market has recently been called a bubble by several authorities on the matter. Whether or not it really is a bubble is another debate, but, if it is, this will have a huge impact on consumers, regardless of whether or not they choose to use AI. Overspending is a big problem, and if the market suddenly re-adjusts itself, inflation may rise rapidly, banks may fail, and the government may need to overspend in response. One of the until-now-unforseen advantages of open source distribution would be a relaxation of market forces. 

Even if all of the spending on AI does lead to a return on value, it doesn't change the fact that right now AI remains a highly speculative market. Nvidia, the leading producer of AI training chips, has seen serious profits since Chat-GPT's big debut, but its price-to-earning ratio remains quite high at 45. Furthermore, many companies are relying on debt to stay in-line with the competition: for example, Oracle is predicted to triple its debt by 2030 if it continues to leverage it to invest in AI \[8\]. All of this speculation leads to a more volatile market, a market that is more hostile to the average individual participant. If fully open-source, competitive AI models were released, it would ease some of that pressure. It would be easier for investors to determine what the true capabilities of AI are without all of the conflicting and biased marketing from the tech companies that produce the models, and those companies would no longer feel it necessary to rely on debt just to stay in the race. Otherwise, the overvaluation of AI shares, if it exists, will only worsen, and, if history is anything to go by, it is consumers that will pay the price.

### AI Could Be Cheaper
"But AI is already free!" you might say, having used powerful models on your own personal computer. It's true that free access to AI has been the norm so far, but this is, perhaps, misleading. When you pay to use proprietary Adobe editing software, you are also paying for Adobe's access to industry models that power its new AI tools: this is how big tech is making much of its money on AI \[4\]. If a certain AI-powered feature becomes more or less indespensible for a given type of state of the art software, then open source versions, say, Krita in the case of Adobe Photoshop, will become obsolete, particularly for the companies that might otherwise fund these open source projects. Thus, the consumer will pay for proprietary AI even if it is not a direct transaction. Continuing with the graphic art example, freely available models like Deepseek make it easier for the Krita foundation to include AI features in their software. They still need to find a place to host those models, but once they find that hardware, all they need to do is transfer the Deepseek model to it, and all their users will have access. 

### Efficiency and the Environment
Capitalism motivates innovation in products as far as they increase value. Thus, regardless of the state of open source AI, big tech will continue to develop better and better models for consumers to use. However, this does not necessarily apply to efficiency. Once a company has purchased the necessary hardware, its utilization is relatively inexpensive, and there is minimal motivation to make the same model train more efficiently. Open source AI developers will never have access to the same initial investment that big tech has access to, so independent producers must find ways to make training these models more efficient. Deepseek's quality is not beyond that of industry models; rather, what makes it ground-breaking is its innovations in training efficiency; through methods like using a "mixture of experts," Deepseek requires far less initial hardware to train \[4\]. This does not benefit the consumer directly, but, in the future, AI companies will use these innovative methods and the whole industry will have a smaller environmental impact. That, of course, *will* benefit consumers by slowing climate warming.

### Balancing the Risks
Of course, many of the dangers mentioned in the last section will affect consumers. In particular, AI may pose a danger to its own user: by having access to models without safeguards, users may find themselves taken to dark places by their AI companion. Their extremist impulses may be encouraged rather than avoided, and, without any regard for content, the AI may go so far as to provide them with the information necessary to carry out violent crimes, like generating instructions for how to 3D print a gun or writing a recipe for deadly chemicals \[5\]. And, naturally, all consumers could possibly be targeted by mass election meddling or be made into deepfaked pornography without ever touching AI themselves. 

Yet, even taking that all at face value, blocking open source AI comes with its own risks to national security. When Meta released an open-weighted Llama AI in 2023, Chinese models quickly became predicated on it, and the United States was thusly made the dominant power in AI production. Now, Deepseek's impressiveness means that the reverse may occur, with even moderate-sized US technology companies and research teams building their models on top of Chinese platforms. This will not only put China in a dominant position to ride the economic AI boom (with less risks should it turn bubbly), but also create an easy opportunity for the Chinese government to infiltrate much of the USA's technology infrastructure \[4\]. Perhaps closer integration of US and Chinese technology could improve relationships between the two powers, but, nontheless, consumers should be aware of what could happen to them should their products be built on Chinese tech going forward.

### Conclusion
In a sense, contemporary generative AI cannot be fully open sourced even if its developers wanted to do so. AI is a black box system, meaning that no one knows exactly how its structure leads it to the outputs it gives: it is opaque. In this sense, the difference between open and closed source AI may not mean much to consumers: even if it were open source, no one would be able to understand it. However, for developers, it could mean a great deal.


## Stakeholder 2: Developers
### Speaking from Personal Experience
As a developer myself, this is a stakeholder for which I can speak anecdotally. In fact, I have and very likely will go on to doing research that involves artificial intelligence, and open sourceness will be very important for my work. The only reason I or any other independent or academic developer has been able to contribute to AI research is open source frameworks. Much of AI, including in the industry, is run with PyTorch now, and the PyTorch foundation uses the open source BSD 3-Clause licence for their library. This means that when researchers share there methods, even if there is no source code, only one implementation is required for the entire developer community to use it. I worked on a project that used graph neural networks to reason about SMT queries, and we were able to work with state of the art graph attention layers because their implementation is freely available. I would not have been able to participate in that part of the project otherwise!

### Impact on Early Developers
Similarly, open sourse software has been hugely beneficial for the inclusivity of software development everywhere. An example that I love is Godot. Many computer scientists begin their journey as young children because they want to learn how to make video games, and, while powerful, free software like Unity or Unreal Engine exists, it is bloated and difficult to use for beginners, there are less resources available for getting help, and it is not something that can be easily contributed to. With the Godot foundation's introduction of Godot 4, video game development is fully democratized: this is an engine that is more intuitive to use by independent developers because it was built by the same, and it benefits from rapid introduction of optional features from its own passionate user base. There is also a natural way for users to get into real software development: they run into a simple feature requirement the engine lacks, and they learn how to contribute to the project in order to incorporate that feature according to their vision. This is a fantastic experience for a young developer to have! All in all, having a powerful open source version of a particular kind of software available encourages healthy, long-term growth in not only that specific field, but also computer science as a whole.

Artificial intelligence is another area that I believe is appealing to inexperienced developers. Of course, purchasing hardware will always cost money, but the more open and available model training code is, the more opportunity early developers will have to solve their own problems with machine learning. 

### Making Industry-Relevant Experience More Available
These days, every software engineering job listing at a major tech or tech-adjacent company will describe the ways they are incorporating AI workflows into their development process. Just last week, I was frustrated to see that Mayo Clinic was looking for entry level employees with years of experience with specific, expensive AI tools. AI may be free to use for consumers in general, but industry-standard development tools usually cost money that I don't want to spend just so I can check a box. It's the same situation as cloud computing: by moving the majority of software infrastructure to Amazon, Azure, or GCP's servers, companies make it more difficult for fresh graduates to meet their expectations of experience. Unlike cloud computing, free access to AI tools is possible if free, state of the art models can be used for the introduction of free development tools. That would make it easier for these tools to be incorporated into curriculums as well. Personally, I hope this future comes to pass, even if I won't be able to take advantage of it.

## Conclusion: The Future, Open Source as Ideology
Open source has more applications than just software. The book *Open Source Law, Policy, and Practice*, a primary resource for this case study, is itself open source: that is, free to access and distribute. Open access research publications have become more and more popular and desirable, with researchers fighting for the maximum open access period a journal will allow so that there work can be more visible. And, it is not just the source for intellectual property that can be made free and distributable. Open source software has been the focus of this case study, but open source hardware exists too: the design of Arduino microcontrollers is freely accessible, and it is even free to produce the devices and sell them yourself \[1\]. 

Of course, open source does not apply to physical objects so naturally as it does to software because producing the hardware from its source (its design) is a lot more expensive than for software, but that doesn't make the premise absurd. One reason that open source hardware is becoming more meaningful now is improved simulation technology. In open source software development, the design, build, test cycle is critical to nearly all relevant methodologies (Agile, for example). In hardware development, this cycle is a lot more difficult for an open source community. Complex, accurate, and robust simulation provides an alternative to the build step, making the development of hardware more approachable \[1\]. Improved access to useful simulation software is made easier by its open sourceness, so there is reason to believe that the open source method can make itself more prevalent over time. 

But wait! There's more! Open source can be applied to governance as well. This is more than a metaphor for democracy: accountability is a problem in all forms of governmenment, and requiring openness could be the solution \[Brock\]. When the US invaded Iraq on the pretext of weapons of mass destruction (which did not exist), it did so based on extremely faulty evidence. It's not that there was some secret piece of motivating information that was not made open source; rather, the very fact that the government is not required to make information open source allowed them to convince the public that they had additional information. The point is, the open source method can be applied to more than software. Other applications require different licences -- for example, creative content is often distributed as open under a creative commons licence, which does not distinguish between object or source code -- but the open source idea unifies them all. The ethics and usefulness of this would, of course, require their own case study to unravel, but, in a system where every piece of information is ruthlessly appropriated for profit, open source offers one glimmer of hope in a natural transition to a more collaborative means of production.
