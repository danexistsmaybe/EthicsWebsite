## Conclusion: The Future, Open Source as Ideology
Open source has more applications than just software. The book *Open Source Law, Policy, and Practice*, a primary resource for this case study, is itself open source: that is, free to access and distribute. Open access research publications have become more and more popular and desirable, with researchers fighting for the maximum open access period a journal will allow so that there work can be more visible. And, it is not just the source for intellectual property that can be made free and distributable. Open source software has been the focus of this case study, but open source hardware exists too: the design of Arduino microcontrollers is freely accessible, and it is even free to produce the devices and sell them yourself \[1\]. 

Of course, open source does not apply to physical objects so naturally as it does to software because producing the hardware from its source (its design) is a lot more expensive than for software, but that doesn't make the premise absurd. One reason that open source hardware is becoming more meaningful now is improved simulation technology. In open source software development, the design, build, test cycle is critical to nearly all relevant methodologies (Agile, for example). In hardware development, this cycle is a lot more difficult for an open source community. Complex, accurate, and robust simulation provides an alternative to the build step, making the development of hardware more approachable \[1\]. Improved access to useful simulation software is made easier by its open sourceness, so there is reason to believe that the open source method can make itself more prevalent over time. 

But wait! There's more! Open source can be applied to governance as well. This is more than a metaphor for democracy: accountability is a problem in all forms of governmenment, and requiring openness could be the solution \[Brock\]. When the US invaded Iraq on the pretext of weapons of mass destruction (which did not exist), it did so based on extremely faulty evidence. It's not that there was some secret piece of motivating information that was not made open source; rather, the very fact that the government is not required to make information open source allowed them to convince the public that they had additional information. The point is, the open source method can be applied to more than software. Other applications require different licences -- for example, creative content is often distributed as open under a creative commons licence, which does not distinguish between object or source code -- but the open source idea unifies them all. The ethics and usefulness of this would, of course, require their own case study to unravel, but, in a system where every piece of information is ruthlessly appropriated for profit, open source offers one glimmer of hope in a natural transition to a more collaborative means of production.

## Discussion Questions
1. Have you used open source software in your personal life, for a job, or for school? If so, do you feel like the fact that it was open source benefited you, and if not, is there any software you use that you think would benefit from being open source? How so?
2. The reliance of startups on the hardware of big tech makes it difficult for them to be independent. What problems does the overwhelming influence of the four or five biggest tech companies on the development of AI have for the industry? Could open source AI limit these effects?
3. Is there any way to democratize the use of hardware in the same way that open source distribution democratizes software development?
4. Is Deepseek a threat to US interests or an opportunity to diversify the AI industry? If it is both, which is more important from the perspective of a US policy-maker?
5. What makes open source machine learning different from open source software, and what changes to open source software licenses should be made to account for those differences?
6. Can the public and academic sectors keep up with the industry's development AI? Should the government use the law to position itself as more involved in the development process, thus encouraging more open source practices?
7. Do you think "secure" AI would be effective in limiting bad actors, and do the dangers of open source AI concern you? Do they outweigh the benefits? What experiences or events inform your opinion?
8. The influence of AI on the domestic market resembles that of the prelude to the dot-com bubble which burst in the year 2000, a bubble that was largely created due to over-eager spending on and investment in any technology products that had verbal signifiers of leveraging the internet. Do you see this instance as different? Despite the bubble, the internet remains as a hugely influential part of everyday life. Would the establishment of AI be worth a global recession?
9. If you are a developer, how have your experiences been shaped by open source software? Do you think your journey would have been easier if more software was open source?
10. In a recently given speech, Linus Torvalds, developer of the open source Linux operating system and the open source DevOps management software Git, said, 
> “In the tech industry, so much is about the hype. Everybody is following everybody else like lemmings off a cliff, trying to chase the next big thing, and I don’t think that’s a successful strategy. I think you need to find something that isn’t what everybody else does and excel at that and be the first to do something slightly different.” 
Does open source development have a place in "hype" areas of tech? Do you feel motivated to participate in open source? In what ways can open source distribution be incentivized? 
11. Is there anything outside of software that you can imagine open source being applied to? What makes it more difficult, and are there any solutions? Would it be productive?
