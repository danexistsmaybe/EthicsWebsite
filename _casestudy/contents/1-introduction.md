## Introduction
Across discussions around open source software, particularly from its proponents and contributers, the word *community* comes up quite frequently. Community is, in many ways, a utopian word, a word that invokes all of the most beautiful parts of social relations while somehow navigating the difficulties. At its most abstract, the quality of being open source represents the realization of a modern, serious effort towards that utopian ideal and an indication that technology is useful, perhaps even essential, for getting there. Nevertheless, the first free and open source software (FOSS) was not written with such grand intentions; the reality of open source software takes a much more specific and nuanced form; and critics of such an idea claim that it is unprofitable, vulnerable, and ultimately unsustainable. After providing an interactive history of FOSS, I will examine the most recent development in its timeline, generative AI. I will go on to provide analysis of FOSS from the perspectives of the two biggest stakeholders, consumers and developers. Finally, I will discuss the economic reality of FOSS, and I will then conclude with the notion of open source as a movement and an ideal, ending with an argument that FOSS provides a clear framework towards realizing our collective hopes as a society.

### Background
Before all that, I must provide some background on the case. Traditional models of software distribution rely on the fact that the process of compilation is difficult to reverse and can easily be further obfuscated without affecting performance. In other words, given a piece of compiled machine code, it is impossible to recover its readability without difficult and time-consuming interpretation and refactoring. Thus, for-profit distributors of software have most often released their programs as binary code, which is effective in allowing them to continue monetizing their product, keeping it proprietary, and controlling its use.

In simple terms, free and open source software refers to the decision to distribute software as source code rather than as machine code \[Brock\]. There are many different, sometimes conflicting motivations for this decision, including philisophical, ethical, economic, and pragmatic reasoning, but, in each case, distributors must determine how and to what extent they will protect their intellectual property. Controlling the source code is most often accomplished through private law, that is, legally binding agreements between creators and consumers like those expressed in common open source software licenses.

### Open Source AI
The most recent development in the area of FOSS is, of course, generative AI. The vast majority of state-of-the-art AI models in the United States have been kept under lock and key, only accessible through web interfaces and API calls. Some have read this as a sign of open source's inability to remain useful in the face of modern developments in computational infrastructure, while others have argued that moving to an open source model for AI development would pose to great of a security risk \[Danger Article\]. Either way, it was widely accepted as simple reality that generative AI models only existed behind the closed doors of big tech companies like Google and Meta and well-funded startups like OpenAI and Anthropic. That is, until DeepSeek released its open weight v3 model in December of 2024 \[NYT article\]. The introduction of a free, competitive model from China has significantly disrupted previous narratives around the development of AI, and the industry continues to move forward with many open questions.

Still, in order to understand current discourse over open source AI, it is necessary to understand how FOSS began, what events led to its popularity, and how more recent developments in the software development landscape have changed the way FOSS is performed in industry.
