## Stakeholder 1: Consumers
### Open Source and the AI Bubble
The artificial intelligence market has recently been called a bubble by several authorities on the matter. Whether or not it really is a bubble is another debate, but, if it is, this will have a huge impact on consumers, regardless of whether or not they choose to use AI. Overspending is a big problem, and if the market suddenly re-adjusts itself, inflation may rise rapidly, banks may fail, and the government may need to overspend in response. One of the until-now-unforseen advantages of open source distribution would be a relaxation of market forces. 

Even if all of the spending on AI does lead to a return on value, it doesn't change the fact that right now AI remains a highly speculative market. Nvidia, the leading producer of AI training chips, has seen serious profits since Chat-GPT's big debut, but its price-to-earning ratio remains quite high at 45. Furthermore, many companies are relying on debt to stay in-line with the competition: for example, Oracle is predicted to triple its debt by 2030 if it continues to leverage it to invest in AI \[8\]. All of this speculation leads to a more volatile market, a market that is more hostile to the average individual participant. If fully open-source, competitive AI models were released, it would ease some of that pressure. It would be easier for investors to determine what the true capabilities of AI are without all of the conflicting and biased marketing from the tech companies that produce the models, and those companies would no longer feel it necessary to rely on debt just to stay in the race. Otherwise, the overvaluation of AI shares, if it exists, will only worsen, and, if history is anything to go by, it is consumers that will pay the price.

### AI Could Be Cheaper
"But AI is already free!" you might say, having used powerful models on your own personal computer. It's true that free access to AI has been the norm so far, but this is, perhaps, misleading. When you pay to use proprietary Adobe editing software, you are also paying for Adobe's access to industry models that power its new AI tools: this is how big tech is making much of its money on AI \[4\]. If a certain AI-powered feature becomes more or less indespensible for a given type of state of the art software, then open source versions, say, Krita in the case of Adobe Photoshop, will become obsolete, particularly for the companies that might otherwise fund these open source projects. Thus, the consumer will pay for proprietary AI even if it is not a direct transaction. Continuing with the graphic art example, freely available models like Deepseek make it easier for the Krita foundation to include AI features in their software. They still need to find a place to host those models, but once they find that hardware, all they need to do is transfer the Deepseek model to it, and all their users will have access. 

### Efficiency and the Environment
Capitalism motivates innovation in products as far as they increase value. Thus, regardless of the state of open source AI, big tech will continue to develop better and better models for consumers to use. However, this does not necessarily apply to efficiency. Once a company has purchased the necessary hardware, its utilization is relatively inexpensive, and there is minimal motivation to make the same model train more efficiently. Open source AI developers will never have access to the same initial investment that big tech has access to, so independent producers must find ways to make training these models more efficient. Deepseek's quality is not beyond that of industry models; rather, what makes it ground-breaking is its innovations in training efficiency; through methods like using a "mixture of experts," Deepseek requires far less initial hardware to train \[4\]. This does not benefit the consumer directly, but, in the future, AI companies will use these innovative methods and the whole industry will have a smaller environmental impact. That, of course, *will* benefit consumers by slowing climate warming.

### Balancing the Risks
Of course, many of the dangers mentioned in the last section will affect consumers. In particular, AI may pose a danger to its own user: by having access to models without safeguards, users may find themselves taken to dark places by their AI companion. Their extremist impulses may be encouraged rather than avoided, and, without any regard for content, the AI may go so far as to provide them with the information necessary to carry out violent crimes, like generating instructions for how to 3D print a gun or writing a recipe for deadly chemicals \[5\]. And, naturally, all consumers could possibly be targeted by mass election meddling or be made into deepfaked pornography without ever touching AI themselves. 

Yet, even taking that all at face value, blocking open source AI comes with its own risks to national security. When Meta released an open-weighted Llama AI in 2023, Chinese models quickly became predicated on it, and the United States was thusly made the dominant power in AI production. Now, Deepseek's impressiveness means that the reverse may occur, with even moderate-sized US technology companies and research teams building their models on top of Chinese platforms. This will not only put China in a dominant position to ride the economic AI boom (with less risks should it turn bubbly), but also create an easy opportunity for the Chinese government to infiltrate much of the USA's technology infrastructure \[4\]. Perhaps closer integration of US and Chinese technology could improve relationships between the two powers, but, nontheless, consumers should be aware of what could happen to them should their products be built on Chinese tech going forward.

### Conclusion
In a sense, contemporary generative AI cannot be fully open sourced even if its developers wanted to do so. AI is a black box system, meaning that no one knows exactly how its structure leads it to the outputs it gives: it is opaque. In this sense, the difference between open and closed source AI may not mean much to consumers: even if it were open source, no one would be able to understand it. However, for developers, it could mean a great deal.
