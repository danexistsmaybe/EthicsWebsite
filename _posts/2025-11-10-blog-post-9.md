**Article**  
[Censorship of Misinformation and Freedom of Speech on Social Media](https://mit-serc.pubpub.org/pub/lkf63cu5/release/1)

<hr style="background-color: #000; border: none; height: 2px">

In *Censorship of Misinformation and Freedom of Speech on Social Media*, Kevin A. I. Mills describes current discourse surrounding what has been called "the disinformation age" or an "epistemic crisis," both substantiating this fear and calling the idea that it is a purely recent phenomenon into question. He goes on to analyze the current state of US American media as the result of a historical trajectory, and ends with the conclusion that censorship is not the answer to the availability of misinformation.

## Misinformation Has Not Gotten Worse, Populism Has
In this case study, Mills challenges the notion that misinformation has gotten worse, yet ultimately concedes that the deterioration of "high modernist" reporting has led to media that is less well-founded in truth, even if "high modernism" was not as objective as it purported to be. I would argue that this is false; I am not so confident that misinformation can be so readily quantified and compared, for several of the same reasons Mills mentions at the beginning of his study. Mainly, I see a constant stream of misinformation over the last fifty years -- which is the time frame used in the article -- from misinformation about the Soviet threat at the end of World War II, to misinformation about Cuba and the Vietnam War, then to misinformation about Iraq and the wars on drugs and terror, all the way to the present issues we face today, all of which suggests to me that misinformation really is something of a constant. In fact, I would argue that focusing on the state of misinformation is to ignore the real issue, which is populism. 

The United States, fat off of its imperial exploits and dominant capitalist production, has not experienced populism to its current extent because its people did not feel the rubber of authority stamping down on them until now. The myth of objective reporting was believable because a dialectic between the people and a small elite was not on so many people's minds. Mills claims that conspiracy theories are nothing new, which is true, but he overlooks the advanced hold they have recently formed on popular imagination. There are not more conspiracies, they are simply more compelling, and this is because of new found populism. Whether or not populism is strictly bad is a different discussion, but I think it is reasonable to say that contemporary issues of misinformation have sprung up from a new found populism, not because there are more or less facts on the information market.

## Applying Censorship to Social Media
I agree with Mills that censorship is not an effective means of exclusively suppressing misinformation, even though I do think it has been proven, both in this country and in others, that censorship *is* an effective way to control a narrative. If it were effective at preventing misinformation, however, I absolutely do think its use would be justified. If the majority of faulty evidence could so easily be eliminated just by enforcing its removal, perhaps democracy would work the way it was intended to. In this fictional world, I would advise taking censorship to the fullest extent possible, in all cases, in order to finally straighten out the answers to all social problems. However, as Mills describes, objectivity and factual reporting are impossible ideals; in order to be able to censor misinformation, there would need to be some truly unbiased force capable of sorting all information into the categories of "true" and "false," and I do not think I need to explain why this would be a foolish endeavor in our messy, material world.

## Everyone Can Spread Their Information
Digital technologies have certainly catalyzed information flow in this country, in a dramatic way that I would describe as flood gates being opened. Nevertheless, as Mills puts it, "whether \[digital technologies\] have had net-negative or net-positive effects is no simple matter," and I don't think it makes sense to apply an aggregative judgement on these changes. What I can say is that, by dispersing the flow of information, that flow has essentially been democratized, and this has the effect of misrepresenting minority voices *and* reflecting demographic power imbalances that still exist. For example, people of color can, perhaps, put their voices out into the public sphere at a greater volume than ever before, but those voices are invariable going to be drowned out by the voice of the majority (and more powerful) white population.  

## Epistemology
Finally, we arrive at epistemology itself. In a transient, chaotic, and unpredictable world, how can we know anything at all? Philosophy gives us two ways to arrive at knowledge: inductive and deductive reasoning. Inductive reasoning is knowing something through logic. Math proofs and syllogisms fall under this category. Deductive reasoning is all about knowing things through observation; Darwin is widely credited with making it cool again. The thing about inductive reasoning is that it always requires basic assumptions, axioms, and that is why all philosophers that have tried to construct a complete understanding of the world have failed, including Descartes, one of the more famous inductive philosophers. There are many challenges with deductive reasoning as well, such as not being able to observe the future, or all of the challenges around designing independent and random samples that we talk about in statistics. 

To go back to Descartes, he tried to prove that it is possible to know things without considering the trustworthiness of one's surroundings through the famous claim, "I think therefore I am." Many philosophers have criticized the truth of this clause, calling it cyclical, dependent on a pre-defined "I," and so on. Personally, I don't really care whether or not Descartes' argument makes sense. I think he and everyone who overextends themselves on trustworthiness are wasting their time on a useless discourse. Even if I do not know it with 100% certainty, I am confident that people are being slaughtered in Gaza at this very moment. When faced with a possibility like that, spending thought arguing whether or not you can really "know" anything seems a useless activity.

## Discussion Question
What is critical theory, and how can one use it to navigate the overwhelming and uncertain flood of information that is present online?

For me, critical theory is one reliable tool that I have used to continue moving forward and making decisions in the face of uncertainty. I would highly recommend it to anyone else that finds the digital media landscape difficult to navigate. A key part of the critical approach is to accept that most information is not trustworthy-per-se, while leveraging that fact to obtain a more wholistic, action-oriented view of information. I would really like to see others engage with this method, which is why I would ask this question.

## Reflection
This was an interesting case study because I think a lot about knowledge, the media, and censorship. I think it helped me organize my thoughts around populism in the US. However, I fear that it might be blanketly problematic that the author, whether he means to or not, ends up defending Elon Musk's claim that he is "freeing Twitter" from censorship. I wish he had specifically detailed how Musk fails to do that, and why that statement is also wrapped up in issues of hate speech, a slightly different concern than misinformation (which Mills does point out, but only very briefly).
