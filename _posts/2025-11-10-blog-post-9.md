**Article**  
[Censorship of Misinformation and Freedom of Speech on Social Media](https://mit-serc.pubpub.org/pub/lkf63cu5/release/1)

<hr style="background-color: #000; border: none; height: 2px">

In *Censorship of Misinformation and Freedom of Speech on Social Media*, Kevin A. I. Mills describes current discourse surrounding what has been called "the disinformation age" or an "epistemic crisis," both substantiating this fear and calling the idea that it is a purely recent phenomenon into question. He goes on to analyze the current state of US American media as the result of a historical trajectory, and ends with the conclusion that censorship is not the answer to the availability of misinformation.

### Misinformation Has Not Gotten Worse, Populism Has
In this case study, Mills challenges the notion that misinformation has gotten worse, yet ultimately concedes that the deterioration of "high modernist" reporting has led to media that is less well-founded in truth, even if "high modernism" was not as objective as it purported to be. I would argue that this is false; I am not so confident that misinformation can be so readily quantified and compared, for several of the same reasons Mills mentions at the beginning of his study. Mainly, I see a constant stream of misinformation over the last fifty years -- which is the time frame used in the article -- from misinformation about the Soviet threat at the end of World War II, to misinformation about Cuba and the Vietnam War, then to misinformation about Iraq and the wars on drugs and terror, all the way to the present issues we face today, all of which suggests to me that misinformation really is something of a constant. In fact, I would argue that focusing on the state of misinformation is to ignore the real issue, which is populism. 

The United States, fat off of its imperial exploits and dominant capitalist production, has not experienced populism to its current extent because its people did not feel the rubber of authority stamping down on them until now. The myth of objective reporting was believable because a dialectic between the people and a small elite was not on so many people's minds. Mills claims that conspiracy theories are nothing new, which is true, but he overlooks the advanced hold they have recently formed on popular imagination. There are not more conspiracies, they are simply more compelling, and this is because of new found populism. Whether or not populism is strictly bad is a different discussion, but I think it is reasonable to say that contemporary issues of misinformation have sprung up from a new found populism, not because there are more or less facts on the information market.
