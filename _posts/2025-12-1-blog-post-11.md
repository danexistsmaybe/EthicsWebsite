**Article**  
[Complete Delete: In Practice, Clicking 'Delete' Rarely Deletes. Should it?](https://mit-serc.pubpub.org/pub/fesqymtr/release/3?readingCollection=ca73f7c0)

<hr style="background-color: #000; border: none; height: 2px">

Simson Garfinkel, in his publication *Complete Delete: In Practice, Clicking 'Delete' Rarely Deletes. Should it?*, discusses the possibilities of deletion in the information age. He begins with what happens when one deletes a file in the present, then describes some of the history of file storage, and finally ends with a discussion on the different approaches and viewpoints that society could take going forward.

## Users Should Be Given as Much Information as Possible
Most people would agree that they want to be kept in the loop in regards to the effectiveness of functions given to them in a digital system. On the other hand, informing users of the myriad of ways in which their data may remain after they click delete presents a common challenge of interface design, which is that (i) users often feel more comfortable with a minimalist, uncluttered design and may even feel threatened when disclaimer and warning messages appear, and (ii) providing more information does not always clarify things, particularly when that information requires technical knowledge to fully understand. It seems that Garfinkel believes users have only gotten stupider over the years, saying, "Although engineers once hoped that a new generation of ‘digital natives’ who grew up with advanced technology would have no problems using these complex systems, that turned out to be wishful thinking." Nevertheless, I tend to think that it is better to give as much information as possible for those that can understand it. One excellent user experience invention that helps limit the damage of this approach is the "do not show me this again" checkbox. Every time a user selects the delete option for a file, the system could present a little popup to the user declaring where copies of the file have been made and which ones will be deleted, and if this is bothersome the user can simply select "do not show me this again" and then change it later in settings.

## Permanent Deletion is Not so Important Anyways
Garfinkel writes much of his case study under the assumption that the inability to fully delete data is a great source of anxiety for many users, without doing a very good job explaining why. Their argument primarily rests on the sentence, "Computer security experts have long understood that residual data poses a threat to both privacy and security," and then they bring up the notion of sextortion. The thing about sextortion is that the sort of subtle difference between pseudo- and complete-deletion that is being discussed in the article doesn't really have a bearing on that issue. It's not like the perpetrators of sextortion are hacking into their victim's iCloud to find sensitive images; typically, these sorts of unwanted copies are created with screenshot functionality, or saving an image from a messaging app onto one's own device. That is, perhaps, an important conversation to have, but it's not so relevant to our discussion.

More broadly, I've never understood the narrative that lingering data need be threatening to any average software user, and Garfinkel is not able to convince me either. If anything, I'm more in the camp that if designers can avoid permament deletion, they should, simply because previously irrelevant information may become useful in the future. There are security exceptions, of course, but I think that as programmers and policy-makers continue to develop large consumer-grade systems, it will be more useful to build those systems around the fact that information may be difficult to remove, rather than desparately trying to stamp out any possibility of incomplete deletion occurring. Security protocols like 2-factor authentication are good examples of how systems can protect their users without knowing what exactly those users are doing with their information.

## The Anti-Freedom Boogeymen
Frankly, the argument that a backdoor for the US would mean a backdoor for China, Russia, or Iran is xenophobic and politically ignorant. This double standard cannot be taken seriously after all of the foreign intervention, wartime attrocities, and exploitation committed by the United States around the world. Of course, I don't think that means the FBI and other internal security organizations should have a backdoor into everyone's data. It's just that it is all the more disturbing when the only thing standing between the United States government and the private data of its citizens is some horrible, hateful notion that the governments of other countries are intrinsically evil.

## Let People Make Their Own Mistakes, But Don't Mislead Them
Garfinkel never really says it outright, but the position of his article seems to be that it is not up to a company to protect people from their own stupidity, and I agree. It is my belief that if a user signs up for a cloud backup services, deletes a problematic image on their device, and then a family member finds it in a shared google photo drive later, that's their problem. On the other hand, the average user cannot be expected to understand that a file might exist as unlabeled data until it is overwritten, or the distinction between permanent and volatile memory, and so on, and they might even be justified if they didn't know their phone was automatically backing up their files without their input. When should we hold users responsible for their ignorance? 

Certainly when they attempt to redact information on a PDF by drawing over it. If redacting that information were truly important to them, they would determine whether or not drawing over it were a sufficient method, and it is not difficult to find out that it is not. Deleting files is a little different because the language implies one thing while the functionality does not carry through. A marker in a PDF editing application does not purport to be capable of true redaction. I believe this is why so many cloud services will use the word "remove" instead of delete now, even after a file has been removed from trash. That word can be reasonably considered accurate whether the file is being truly deleted or not. In fact, if we replaced all use of the word delete with remove in consumer software applications, I doubt this case study would exist at all. 

## Discussion Question
**What is a sensitive piece of information that you would like to delete? Please submit your answer to evans11@stolaf.edu.**

I am asking this question because I would like to collect people's sensitive information.


Okay, here's the real question:
**When a person takes a screenshot of an image created or uploaded by someone else, who does the screenshot belong to? If it belongs to the screenshottee, how could they be allowed to delete that screenshotted image? If it belongs to the screenshotter, does that make uploading an image online an authorization for others to take ownership over it?**

I ask this question because Garfinkel never really discusses the way that the actions of others can complicate the idea of deletion. Screenshots are just the most obvious, and you can even take this question to the topic of artificial intelligence. On some level, it makes true erasure a futile fantasy, but one could still make attempts at reigning in this ability of others to make copies of someone's data. It also gets at the very idea of intellectual property itself.
