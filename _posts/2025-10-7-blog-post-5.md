### Introduction
Personally, I'm sick of always contributing the same boring Western imperialism every time I wake up in the morning and turn my phone on. Every time I access the internet, that's more money in Google's pocket. Every time I stream music, that's a few more cents going into some US American billionair's pocket. The United States has a monopoly on all our technology; yet, this isn't true for everybody around the world. China is the next biggest player in the tech industry, and, as the US continues to alienate its allies and neighbors, China is becoming more and more of a amiable business partner for much of the world. 

Nevertheless, in this country, information about Chinese tech is so obscured beneath cultural stereotypes, propoganda, and huge disparity of dialogue that it's very hard to know what's really going on. By speaking to a few experts in breaking Chinese technology, the Data & Society Research Institute reveals a little bit of the actual picture, straight from the source.

### The Speakers
In this episode of Databites, Chinese scholar Jing Tsu is brought on to converse with talking points presented by one Julian Gewirtz, co-editor of Logic Magazine’s China Issue. He begins by trying to roughly locate Chinese tech, which he admits is challenging since both US and Chinese firms are often transnational. Moreover, he describes how many are concerned about the Chinese state's use technology as a tool for control. 

Jing Tsu presents China's perspective as a state that sees itself as emergent: that is, emerging onto the global superpower stage. She describes China's initial entry into the global technology market after the Sino-Soviet split in very neoliberal terms -- "pull yourself up by your bootstraps" -- in a way that bothers me. I decided to see if I could find any discourse about her online; as it turns out, she is Taiwanese American, but mostly grew up in the United States. What little discourse I could find exhibited some discomfort with her Western-centric perspective with a deeply set liberal core. This is a little dissapointing, as it doesn't seem to be the genuine look into China's perspective that I was hoping for.

### Main Point
Jing Tsu's primary argument is that the United States' assumption that China would assimilate into or become like the West in terms of its technology and consumer model was unfounded. She says that, while China knew it needed to learn and build off of Western innovation in technology, it never aimed to become the West: its goal was always to forge its own identity. Yet, she does not seem willing to critique this attitude for what it is, which is to say, imperialism. The US conception of its technological positions as superior even for Chinese people is naked idealism, and this belief has never been founded on a material analysis of the Chinese people. 

Her example is the encoding of Chinese characters for digital keyboards. After the cultural revolution, the Chinese government gave a Western computing production firm theoretical character encodings for the development of large-scale commodity production in China. Yet, after some years of complications and whatnot, when the firm came to discuss payment, China refused to shell out any money. She explains that this was because China had realized that US firms could not develop for the needs of Chinese people. A Chinese scholar had already developed the encodings, and China had nothing to gain from letting a US firm reap all of the capital gained by selling this new technology. From then on, China began to focus on developing for itself. She describes how Chinese firms, contrary to public perception in the US, do have competition not just from each other, but also neighboring countries like Vietnam and India. 

In my opinion, this tackles a common misconception among US Americans that Chinese people don't use Western technology platforms because they are not allowed to or because they are backwards and undeveloped. Instead, it seems that, in many cases, platforms like Twitter or Facebook simply don't appeal to Chinese consumers, as they were not developed with that market in mind. It goes the same way with Chinese technology, although there are exceptions like TikTok. TikTok seems like such an important example because it absolutely breaks a lot of these misconceptions: in fact, it is the United States that is banning Chinese platforms from appealing to US consumers, which is quite the paradigm shift.

### New Question
**You have talked about how the cultural, geographical, political, and economic differences between Chinese and US Americans leads to different foci in technology development. How do you see this development intersecting? That is, what specific similarities or shared experiences create demand that could be satisfied by similar products in both countries?**

I would like all discourse surrounding Chinese-American relations to emphasize brotherhood (excuse my use of a gendered term) and commonalities. I don't mean that I would like to advocate for some sort of vapid humanist liberalism, but rather that I think there is value in trying to penetrate the US propaganda efforts to cloak China in the veil of the "other," or, more specifically, as the enemy.

### Conclusion
In the States, China is more often invoked as a domestic weapon of power than in order to have any real, useful conversation about global issues. What I mean is that spreading fear about China's weapons, production, or political ideology is one way politicians exert control over the people. Yet, the material reality is that China is made up of billions of people who are controlled using US threat narratives in the same way. Open conversations like the one in this panel recording help to pierce the barrier separating our nations, however slightly.
