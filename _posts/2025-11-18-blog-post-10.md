**Article**  
[ARTificial: Why Copyright Is Not the Right Policy Tool to Deal with Generative AI](https://yalelawjournal.org/forum/artificial-why-copyright-is-not-the-right-policy-tool-to-deal-with-generative-ai)

<hr style="background-color: #000; border: none; height: 2px">

In *ARTificial: Why Copyright Is Not the Right Policy Tool to Deal with Generative AI*, Micaela Mantegna argues that copyright is not the appropriate avenue for challenging the nonconsentual use of intellectual property in the training of large generative AI models. They begin by outlining the different types of generative AI, particularly text to image architectures, then move to the ethical issues surrounding AI, and finally conclude with their arguments, from multiple perspectives, as to why copyright is not effective.

## Authors Should Not Need to be Motivated by Profit
Though it is quite exhaustive, one thing that Mantegna's essay lacks is an alternative solution to the copyright system. She claims she will cover this in a follow up essay, but in reality, I don't think there is a solution. Under the rule of the government of the United States, issues of property, theft, and ownership are delegated and penalized by the law. At times, Mantegna's essay functions as a good critique of law within a free market, as many of her arguments go beyond copyright specifically and apply themselves to the law in general. For example, the legal system favors consolidated, profit-driven entities like big tech corporations over individuals who must invest surplus time and capital that they do not already have, and this is true for any element of intellectual property law. 

One of her main points is that making copyright law more strict harms the artistic ecosystem, which has historically benefited from intercommunication and a high degree of collaboration. This is true, and I would go further to say that free and open exchange of art cannot be sustained in a system where the primary motivation for artists is to make a living or to accumulate wealth. Companies were already finding ways to exploit the open distribution of art, and generative AI only makes that easier. The only realistic solution is to eliminate the free art market, replacing it with a national platform that supports artists and encourages free exchange and distribution.

## Debating Whether or Not AI Output is Original or Derivative is Not Productive
Mantegna claims that it is not obvious whether or not AI works should be considered original or not, and she goes on to say, "even if one were to accept the premise that GAI model outputs are effectively a collage, neither collages nor remixes are per se illegal." This is true, especially when you consider that the act of human creation is also a continuous process of derivation. A human's style is something that is predicated on so many other humans that came before that it would not be unreasonable to say most human art is derivative. Nevertheless, I think this debate is simply unproductive. A machine is fundementally different than a human. We should not apply human standards for originality to a machine. The image generated by a machine and the collage assembled by a human could, philisophically speaking, be the same thing, but it does no good to be so interested in the ontology of the image. Rather, we should be considering these things in terms of their material effects, and, as Mantegna demonstrates, AI causes material harm to human artists. Originality can be evaluated by different standards for different sources.

## AI is a Tool
I don't believe a generative AI model is entitled to anything, least of all copyright protection. However, I tend to agree with one of the dominant positions outlined in the essay, which is that AI represents some kind of advanced tool rather than an independent creative. Thus, much like an artist that uses Adobe products, an AI output can be distributed and licenced by whoever produced it. Whether or not it is a useful tool is another question...

## All in All: The Law Should Differentiate Between AI and Human Output
It is frustrating that, throughout her article, Mantegna operates on the assumption that a strict ontology of creativity is necessary in order to make conclusions about AI. This strikes me as very typical of US and European tech and legal culture. Essentially, my point is that having different laws surrounding the production of art by machines and the production of art by humans need not create some sort of existential conflict around what it means to be creative. Rather than start with a definition of creativity or originality and creating policy that upholds that definition in all cases, it is better to observe the material issues that affect artists and society and then create policy that will resolve those issues, thus recognizing the plurality of meaning present in every abstract social idea. Furthermore, there is no reason this would interfere with "aesthetic neutrality" because no aesthetic judgement is required. No one needs to say AI generated content is "good" or "bad" in an aesthetic sense in order to hold it to a different standard of originality.

## Discussion Question
**How far should we, as a community, go to avoid stifling the growth and innovation of artificial intelligence technology? Is the fundemental assumption that stifling that growth poses a problem correct?**
Another thing that frustrates me about this essay is that it refuses to critique AI expansionism. I have yet to understand why this technology is in any way revolutionary or exceedingly useful to the improvement of human life. I see it being used far more to inflict violence and maintain social power structures than to further the development of things like medical technology. If anything, stifling the progress of AI might encourage progress in a more important area, like agriculture or urban planning. I think this is an important question to ask because the benefits of AI technology are so often asserted to be true without really considering its effect holistically.
