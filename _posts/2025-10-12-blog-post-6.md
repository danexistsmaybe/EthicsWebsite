*How does the technology garbage impact the global climate environment?*

**Article**  
[From Mining to E-waste: The Environmental and Climate Justice Implications of the Electronics Hardware Life Cycle](https://mit-serc.pubpub.org/pub/w9ht6hue/release/5?readingCollection=ca73f7c0)

<hr style="background-color: #000; border: none; height: 2px">

### Introduction
With every small town in Minnesota chomping at the data center bit, I hear a lot about the climate impact of computing: but, it's all mostly in regards to software. The AI conversation has gotten so large that it has eclipsed conversations about computing hardware, the footprint of which may be less intuitive because of all the factors involved. When it comes to high performance computing, the climate impact can often be summed up in a few numbers: watts of energy burnt per hour, or gallons of water slurped every day, for example. The impact of hardware is just as large but more nuanced, the difficulties of which this case study seeks to explore.

It's not like machine learning isn't about hardware, either: some would say that the AI boom was due more in part to Nvidia's development of research-grade GPUs than to anything OpenAI ever did. Now, every research institution and data company wants their own RTX 6000, so what is the cost of all that? The article examines this issue through three different contexts: mining, socio-economics, and e-waste; these three broad categories cover the entire lifecycle of a computer chip, from its smog-ridden birth to its garbage-heap grave. It also focuses on developing the life cycle assessment (LCA) model for the impact of hardware, in order to build a standardized way of investigating this issue.

### Discussion (Section 1: 1 & 2; Section 3: 1, 2, & ?)
Let's assume, for the sake of discussion, that all we care about is economic development. The case study well describes all of the various ways we will exploit the environment, indigenous lands, and communities in the global South for profit: even then, the inevitable climate catastrophe hits, we suck the land dry of all useful material, and none of its very good for business. The truth is that industry needs climate justice, and it knows it too: throughout history, there have been energy and tech CEOs that beg the government to force them to regulate their businesses. Yet, the system will always exploit the available resources to the maximum extent that is possible, even if every single member of it is aware of their own doom.

If material were being mined from the earth solely for the purpose of constructing solar panels, it might be worthwhile to stimulate production by relaxing regulations. Obviously, that is not what is going on. If it were, if every bit of material were being used for the direct and equitable improvement of human life and sustainability, we would not need children in Brazil to do the labor. The reason so much production is necessary is the equally rapid rate of consumption: a new iphone every year, new computer chips as soon as AMD releases the next model, etc., etc. 

The case study presents the following solutions to the e-waste problem in particular: "extending the useful life of electronics; promoting a CE, the right to repair, and extended producer responsibility; recycling more e-waste; and protecting informal e-waste workers from environmental and health hazards" (Section III). Many of these solutions involve more stringent regulations on big tech companies, particularly enforcing the right to repair, putting ice on the rapid hardware development, and extending the responsibility of producers for their devices. The problem with all of these approaches is the same: not only will the companies themselves resist them at every turn, but anyone that is involved or a stakeholder in the domestic economy will be upset. Since competition is a fundemental axiom of the established economic system, slowing down development in any way damages the entire US economy, not just the profits of individual companies. Considering the very dominant economic role big tech has right now, it's not hard to imagine measures like this causing a broad recession, which would not be popular among the people, even if it is necessary for the survival of our species.

Of course, the issue of e-waste is directly bound up in that of imperialism. The US ships much of its waste to Latin America. How many nationalistic, popular, anti-US governments in that area has the CIA brought down through organized coups or straight up bombing runs? For example, popular leader of Guatemala, Jacobo Árbenz, was ousted in 1954 with direct aid from CIA and the US United Fruit Company, all because he wanted to implement much needed land reform. It seems unlikely that his government would have accepted the e-waste the US sends there now. Later, the Guatemalan government would commit acts of genocide against the indigenous Mayan people, sometimes called the Silver Holocaust; after destabilization like that, it is no surprise that there would be Guatemalans willing to work in the hazardous informal e-waste sector. 

Finally, there is no getting around the facts that every device has a lifetime, or that technology must be upgraded eventually. In order for humanity to make any use of technology, it must turn to CE for sustainable materials. Yet, turning to CE will not mitigate the extraction of natural resources unless supply and demand are enforced. Under the capitalist model, injecting more material into the production of devices will only decrease demand for natural resources if demand for these devices in general is fully met. Yet, as technology superpowers like the US and China race against each other, whipped to a frenzy by big tech companies, this demand will never be fully met. CE is the solution to the e-waste problem, but it can't fulfill that role under current economic conditions.

The question I would ask to stimulate additional discussion on the issue of e-waste is, **At what point does old hardware become truly obsolete? Can we develop strategies for high performance computing that utilize old systems in parallel?** I think this would make for a very interesting, open, and technical research question. Even from a capitalist perspective, the sheer volume of old, unused hardware feels like a missed opportunity; surely the sum of its computing power is worth something?
